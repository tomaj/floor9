One thing on my mind

In early October, the internet had a week where someone rebuilt Adobe's whole suite as open source. That's worth a moment of thinking, wherever you sit.
01 Food for thought: software just became copyable

Developer Brandon Thomas released ArtCraft: seven free, open-source apps written in Rust with Claude, each one a stand-in for an Adobe product.
PhotoCraft Photoshop
VectorCraft Illustrator
FilmCraft Premiere Pro
LightCraft Lightroom
EffectCraft · DesignCraft · PdfCraft After Effects · InDesign · Acrobat

Why he says it's legal: it's a clean-room rebuild. No Adobe code, written from scratch from public documentation and observed behavior. Copying how something behaves isn't copying the code.

Fair caveats: no one has independently checked that claim, patents are a separate question, and the apps are early alpha - the project's own roadmap puts them at roughly a quarter to a third of what a pro needs. Not a Photoshop killer. Yet.

The tool everyone is talking about

The same week, REA - "Reverse Engineer Anything" - blew up on GitHub. It plugs your coding agent (Claude Code, Codex, Cursor) into real reverse-engineering tools like Ghidra and Hopper. Point it at an app - a native binary, an Android APK, an Electron app, a website - and ask "how does this feature work?"

It doesn't hand you the original source code and it doesn't clone apps. It explains a feature, with evidence, so you can rebuild the idea in your own stack. Its own rule: recreate ideas and behavior, not copyrighted code or assets. (Thomas says ArtCraft didn't use REA itself - but it's the same idea.)

So what does it mean for us?

If anyone can study how a feature works and rebuild it in weeks, features stop being a moat.

What's left is what can't be copied: customers, data, trust - and how fast you move. A telco has plenty of the first three. The fourth is the one we keep working on.

Question for you: what would you rebuild if it took a week instead of a year? Reply - I'm genuinely curious.
